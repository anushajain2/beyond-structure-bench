A good crystal structure is not enough

Can the AI models that simulate atoms be trusted to predict how chip materials behave when they heat up? Four widely used models are tested on 33 chip materials against laboratory measurements: they get crystal structure almost exactly right, but how much a material grows when heated is often off by tens of percent.

MACE-MPA-0 places tantalum's lattice within 0.4 % of experiment and its stiffness within about 6 %, yet predicts that it expands 2.8 times as much as it does when heated. The software and the numerics turned out not to be the cause. The model's vibrations change far too strongly with volume: its Grüneisen parameter is 4.1, against 1.6 inferred from experiment.

What we found

Four AI models (MACE-MPA-0, MatterSim, Orb v3 and Meta's UMA) on 33 chip materials, every prediction compared with laboratory measurements, plus a study of why the worst predictions fail.

0.7–0.8 %

Crystal structure is nearly perfect

Average error in crystal size for all four models; stiffness is off by 11–13 %. On these properties the models are almost indistinguishable.

19–36 %

Heating is where the models fail

Average error in thermal expansion at room temperature (UMA 19 %, Orb v3 21 %, MatterSim 24 %, MACE 36 %). Each model fails badly on different materials: MACE on tantalum (+179 %), MatterSim on silicon (+85 %).

7 of 15

Chip stacks are harder still

Layers that grow by different amounts strain a chip. The best model, UMA, gets that mismatch within 25 % for 7 of 15 real stacks; the others for at most 4. MatterSim gets the direction wrong for 5.

γ = 4.1 vs 1.6

The errors trace to how vibrations change with volume

MACE's Grüneisen parameter for tantalum, against experiment. In the worst cases stiffness and heat capacity are right; the models misjudge how their vibrations shift as the crystal expands.

Mostly their own

Not inherited from the training physics

Compared with the DFT method the models learned from, most of the large errors checked are the models' own. Too-soft semiconductors are the exception: they come from DFT itself.

2 blind tests

Predictions written down before the runs

Orb v3 and UMA were run only after predictions about their errors were committed. Some held (accurate silicon gives correct chip-stack directions); some did not, and both are reported.
