How it was built

Building a tool for deciding whether to trust a simulation

The benchmark and the study are the evidence. This page is about the product decisions: who the evidence is for, what it shows, what it deliberately leaves out, and what changed along the way.

The user and the question

The person this is for is a researcher or engineer about to rely on a number from an ML force field: the lattice mismatch between two layers, a candidate barrier's stiffness, how much a film will expand during an anneal. Their question is narrow and practical: for this material and this property, how far off is this model likely to be, and how do I know?

What existed answered a different question. Leaderboards rank models with one score over many materials, and most benchmarks compare models with the DFT data they were trained on. Neither tells you how far off a model is from experiment for the material in front of you, or what to do when no comparison exists.

Decisions

  1. Show evidence, not recommendations. The explorer shows each model's value, the reference and its source, and the error, for one material at a time. The best number is labelled "lowest observed error in this comparison" because the benchmark cannot statistically separate the models, and saying "use model X" would claim more than the data supports.
  2. Make missing results explain themselves. Every empty cell carries a reason: excluded because the model changed the crystal form, dynamically unstable, no experimental reference, the method breaks down at that temperature, or not computed in this release. Coverage is reported next to accuracy for the same reason.
  3. Keep two references side by side. DFT answers "did the model learn its training data?" and experiment answers "what will I actually get?". Showing both is what reveals that closer agreement with DFT does not mean closer agreement with experiment.
  4. Fix the rules before seeing the results. Grading thresholds, references and temperature windows were set before any comparison, and every later change is dated in the configuration with its reason. That makes the grades hard to tune, even unintentionally.
  5. No live inference on the site. Everything you can explore is read from committed results, so it loads instantly, needs no GPU or API key, and shows exactly what was published. Rerunning is a separate, documented path.
  6. Offer only the inputs the evidence supports. Thermal expansion can be explored at 300 K or over each material's pre-registered window, not at an arbitrary temperature, because there is no evidence at arbitrary temperatures.

What I cut

A universal "best model" score; more models and materials added only to raise the count; a chat interface; a temperature slider; and calibrated uncertainty claims. Two models' disagreement does weakly track their error for thermal expansion, but its confidence interval reaches zero, so it is shown as an exploratory signal rather than built into a feature. The molecular-dynamics route to thermal expansion was cut too, after a cross-check showed a barostat bias, and the quasi-harmonic method replaced it.

Catching failures

The most useful engineering decision was to run checks on every model whatever its results looked like. They caught four problems, two of them in the toolchain and two in this project's own code:

  • a model export whose energy depends on the order of its input (invariance check);
  • a barostat that holds the pressure off its target (a fixed-volume cross-check);
  • two frame-convention errors in my stress code (stress against the energy's own derivative);
  • a free-energy defect that made repeated phonon runs disagree by up to 8 % (determinism check).

Each is documented with a reproducer. The two toolkit findings are written up for the kUPS developers.

Feedback changed the product

An outside review of an earlier version found real problems, and they were fixed rather than argued with. The agent interface, an MCP server that lets an AI assistant query the benchmark, used an excluded model export by default, ignored the temperature it was asked about, and described errors outside the tested range as a "lower bound" without basis. It now uses each model's correct backend, matches the comparison to the temperature, says plainly when there is no evidence, and returns the evidence behind every ranking. The review also led to softer wording where the statistics did not support a claim ("not resolved" instead of "tied"), a coverage table, pinned package versions and model checksums, and an explicit comparison with the two closest published benchmarks.

What's next

Orb v3's benchmark-wide thermal expansion with the corrected method; UMA once its licence is granted; and the two toolkit issues, once they have been reviewed. The six-case study leaves two cases open, Orb's silicon and HfO₂, and they stay open until there is evidence to close them.
