A good crystal structure is not enough: investigating thermal-expansion errors in machine-learned force fields

Three widely used ML force fields reproduce the size of chip-material crystals to within 1 %, then miss how much some of them expand on heating by 70–180 %. This is an account of how six of those failures were traced: first ruling out the software and the numerics, then splitting the error into its physical ingredients.

Anusha Jain

A puzzle in tantalum

Tantalum is one of the metals in a chip's wiring: thin tantalum and tantalum-nitride layers line the copper interconnects and stop copper from diffusing into the insulator around it. Ask MACE-MPA-0, a widely used ML force field, about bulk tantalum and its first answers are excellent. The lattice it relaxes to is within 0.4 % of the measured one. Its bulk modulus, a measure of how hard the crystal is to compress, is within about 6 %.

Then ask how much tantalum expands when it is heated. At room temperature MACE predicts a volumetric expansion coefficient of 53 × 10⁻⁶ per kelvin. The measured value is 19 × 10⁻⁶. The model expands tantalum 2.8 times too much.

This is not an isolated case. Across a benchmark of 33 chip materials, the same model overshoots several other metals, while a different model, MatterSim, gets tantalum nearly right but overshoots silicon by 85 % and gallium arsenide by 68 %. A model can get a crystal's structure and stiffness right and still badly mispredict how it expands. Why? And is the failure in the model, or in how it was run?

Why thermal expansion matters

A chip is a stack of thin layers of very different materials: silicon or compound-semiconductor channels, oxide insulators, metal wiring, barrier and contact layers. During manufacturing the stack is heated, often to several hundred degrees, and it heats up again in use. Neighbouring materials that expand by different amounts strain each other, and enough strain cracks, peels or bends a film.

Simulation is increasingly used to screen candidate materials before anyone makes them, so how far a force field's thermal expansion can be trusted matters. One caution: this study computes the expansion of perfect bulk crystals. That is an ingredient of how a film stack behaves, not a prediction of it. Real layers are thin, often polycrystalline, and have interfaces and defects.

What was evaluated

Two pieces of work feed this article, and they should be kept apart.

The benchmark tests MACE-MPA-0, Orb v3 and MatterSim v1 on 33 materials in five classes, for lattice constant, bulk modulus and thermal expansion. Every result is compared with an experimental value, each stored with its source and a short quote, and where possible with the DFT value the models were trained to reproduce. Grading thresholds were fixed before any comparison was made. Thermal expansion is computed in the quasi-harmonic approximation: the crystal's vibration frequencies are computed at nine volumes, and the volume that minimises the free energy at each temperature gives the expansion. Benchmark-wide thermal results exist for all three models (21–22 graded materials each at 300 K). Their average errors are 36 % (MACE), 24 % (MatterSim) and 21 % (Orb); only Orb versus MACE is resolved. They fail in different places.

The six-case study takes Si, GaAs, InP, Cu, Ta and monoclinic HfO₂ and asks what causes each error. These cases were chosen after the benchmark had shown where the models fail, with copper as the case that works for everyone. So the study explains these cases; it does not establish rules that hold beyond them. Orb v3 was added afterwards as a third model on the same six materials, with its settings fixed before its results were seen.

Ruling out the workflow

A large error has three kinds of possible cause: the software that runs the model, the numerical method that turns the model's forces into an expansion coefficient, and the model itself. The first two had to be ruled out before blaming the third.

Is it the software?

The benchmark runs MACE and Orb through CuspAI's open-source kUPS toolkit, using exported versions of the models. If an export changed a model's behaviour, the error would belong to the export. So identical structures, including the slightly displaced atoms a phonon calculation uses, were sent both through kUPS and through each model's official package. For MACE the two agree to 0.01 meV per atom in energy, and the force response to a displaced atom agrees to 0.04 %. For Orb it agrees to 0.3 %. The software is not the cause.

The same check had already disqualified one export. MatterSim's kUPS export returns energies that change when the same atoms are listed in a different order, returns no number at all for some small cells, and is off from the official package by up to 0.7 eV per atom. MatterSim is therefore run through Microsoft's own package throughout, and marked with an asterisk in every figure.

Is it the numerics?

Here the investigation found a real bug, in this project's own code. In a phonon calculation the three acoustic vibrations at the centre of the Brillouin zone should have exactly zero frequency. Numerically they come out at about 10⁻⁷ THz with a random sign, and the free-energy code counted them whenever the sign happened to be positive. That added small random jumps between volumes. The effect only became visible when the same MatterSim calculation for GaAs gave 28.5, 29.9 and 28.7 in three fresh runs. With those modes excluded, the runs agree to 0.1 %. The fix moved the published room-temperature values by a median of 0.7 % and at most 4 %, so it explains none of the large errors, but every number here comes from the corrected run.

The other numerical settings were then varied one at a time. A three times larger atomic displacement and a 14 Å supercell change the room-temperature expansion by 2 % or less, with one exception at 7 %. The range of volumes scanned changes it by up to 9 % for silicon and 12 % for HfO₂. Putting back the unstable modes that the phonon code leaves out changes it by 1 % or less, except for HfO₂. None of this comes near errors of 70–180 %. Not every check was run for every case, to limit compute time, and the study lists which.

The physical ingredients

With the workflow ruled out, the error belongs to the models. To see which part of a model is wrong, the expansion can be written as a product of four physical quantities:

αV ≈ γ CV / (KT V)

  • γ, the Grüneisen parameter, describes how strongly the crystal's vibration frequencies drop as it expands. It is the main measure of the anharmonicity that drives expansion.
  • CV, the heat capacity, is how much vibrational energy the crystal holds at a given temperature.
  • KT, the bulk modulus, is the stiffness: a softer crystal expands more for the same push.
  • V is the volume per atom.

Each ingredient was computed from the model and compared with experiment. Because the terms multiply, each model's error splits into a factor per ingredient (Figure 2).

Heat capacity and volume are within 6 % of experiment in every case, so they cannot explain large errors. The action is in γ and, for some materials, in the stiffness.

  • MACE on tantalum: γ is 4.1 against 1.56. That alone accounts for the 2.8-fold error. Every vibration mode of MACE's tantalum has γ of at least 2.1, above the measured average.
  • The semiconductors (MACE on GaAs; MatterSim on Si, GaAs and InP) fail more subtly. Their optical vibrations are right, with γ within 5 % of measured values. The problem is in the acoustic branches. In these crystals, some shear vibrations at the edge of the Brillouin zone (short-wavelength transverse acoustic modes) soften as the crystal expands. That gives them a negative γ, which partly cancels the expansion. MatterSim's are barely negative (silicon −0.64, against −1.4 measured) or even positive (InP), so the cancellation is lost. On top of that, MatterSim's semiconductors are 17–29 % too soft.
  • MACE gets silicon right partly by luck: its γ is 21 % too low and its bulk modulus 11 % too soft, and the two errors largely cancel.
  • Orb separates shared failures from model-specific ones. Its acoustic modes in the semiconductors are clearly negative, as they should be. Its overshoots there (+25 % for GaAs, +48 % for InP) come mostly from a bulk modulus 22–25 % too soft, the same softness MatterSim shows. A too-high γ for tantalum, meanwhile, is shared with MACE in a smaller form: Orb's γ is 40 % too high, MACE's 160 %.

An honest limit on these numbers. The experimental γ is not measured directly. It is inferred from the measured expansion, stiffness and heat capacity using the same relation. So a γ ratio says what is left over once stiffness, heat capacity and volume are accounted for. The direct checks on γ are a handful of measured mode values: two acoustic modes in silicon and an optical mode in GaAs and InP. Whether the errors are the models' own or inherited from the DFT data they learned from is not tested here. And the InP reference is a single value averaged over 300–910 K, not a measurement at 300 K, so the InP numbers are the least certain of the six.

A shortcut that failed

Phonon calculations are expensive. A static equation of state, the energy at a few compressed and expanded volumes, is cheap, and a classic relation due to Slater links its pressure derivative K′ to γ: roughly γ ≈ K′/2 − 1/6. MACE's tantalum fits that story. Its K′ is 7.7 against a measured 3.1, an equation of state that stiffens far too fast under compression, consistent with its huge γ. It is tempting to screen models this way and skip the phonons.

Orb breaks the shortcut. Its K′ for tantalum is 3.16, almost exactly the measured 3.07, yet its phonon γ is 40 % too high and its expansion 70 % too large. The γ does not depend on how the displacements were generated or on the export. The static properties are not fully reassuring either, since Orb's bulk modulus for tantalum is 11–14 % soft, but that would explain only about 12–16 % of extra expansion, not 70 %. This is one counterexample, on cases already selected, not a validation. It is enough to say that a normal static equation of state does not certify a model's thermal expansion.

What remains unresolved

Two cases do not resolve cleanly, and it is more useful to say so than to force an explanation.

Orb's silicon lands within 4 % of experiment, but not for a robust reason. Like MACE, Orb's γ is too low and its stiffness too soft. These compensate only partly: the four-ingredient reconstruction is still 22 % low, and the full calculation reaches the right answer only through a 20 % residual. Changing the volume range moves the result from 5.9 to 9.1. A correct number built on shifting ground is a caution, not a success.

HfO₂, the high-k gate oxide, shows all three models with a γ about 28 % too high, partly offset by a 6–9 % stiff bulk modulus. But at 400–1500 K its expansion depends on how unstable interpolated vibrations are treated (+15 to +25 %) and on the volume range (±13 %), and the experimental reference is a provisional compilation. For HfO₂, method and reference uncertainty are as large as the model error.

What to take from it

For anyone using these models: structural accuracy does not certify thermal properties. If expansion matters, compute it, ideally with more than one model, and look at the stiffness and the Grüneisen parameter rather than only the final number. In the benchmark, the average of two models was as accurate as the better model at 300 K and more accurate than either over the temperature windows, and their disagreement weakly tracked the error. That is an exploratory signal, not a calibrated uncertainty.

For anyone building or benchmarking models: the large errors here sit in how vibration frequencies change with volume, a property that training on energies and forces near equilibrium constrains only indirectly. That is a hypothesis to test on other materials, not a conclusion from six cases.

For anyone running simulation workflows: two of the four suspects were real. An export changed a model's behaviour, and this project's own post-processing added noise until a determinism check exposed it. Checks that run whether or not a result looks wrong are what caught both.
