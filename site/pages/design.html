Evaluation design

How the comparisons were built, and how to read them

A benchmark's numbers are only as good as the decisions behind them. These are the questions that shaped this one, each with an example from the results.

Why compare with both DFT and experiment?

They answer different questions. The comparison with DFT (Materials Project, PBE) asks whether a model learned what it was trained on. The comparison with experiment asks what someone using the model would actually get. A model can match DFT closely and still be off from experiment, because PBE itself makes crystals about 1 % too large and somewhat too soft.

The data shows why both are needed. MatterSim reproduces PBE bulk moduli most closely (5.4 % against 9.9 % for MACE), yet against experiment the benchmark cannot tell the two apart. The comparison with DFT is also an in-distribution test, since all three models were trained on DFT data that very likely includes these materials. That is why experiment is the primary reference.

Why several properties instead of one score?

Because models fail differently on different properties, and a single score would hide that. The same models that reproduce lattice constants to under 1 % miss thermal expansion by 70–180 % on some materials. And the errors behave differently: lattice-constant errors are strongly shared between models (correlation 0.83–0.95), consistent with a common bias in the training reference, while thermal-expansion errors are only weakly shared. That is why averaging two models can help for thermal expansion (over the temperature windows it beats both models; at 300 K it only matches the better one) and does nothing for lattice constants.

Why report coverage alongside accuracy?

An average error covers only the cases that worked. Orb v3 relaxes rutile TiO₂, cubic SrTiO₃ and MoS₂ into different crystal forms. Leaving those out of its lattice average is right, since the numbers are not comparable, but it means Orb's average describes 30 materials, not 33. Similarly, MatterSim predicts a mechanically unstable elastic tensor for titanium, and two or three crystals per model are dynamically unstable in the thermal-expansion calculation. Each of these is counted in results/coverage.csv, and every excluded result in the explorer says why it was excluded.

When does the evidence support a ranking?

Only when the difference survives the uncertainty. Each average comes from 17 to 30 materials, so it is reported with a 95 % interval from a bootstrap over materials, with each experimental reference also perturbed by its typical uncertainty. Two models are compared with a paired bootstrap on the materials both have.

Against experiment, no difference between the models is resolved for any property. For bulk modulus, MACE minus Orb is −0.6 percentage points, with an interval of −4.8 to +3.7. That is not evidence that the models are equally good: with this many materials, differences below about 3–5 points would go undetected. Within material classes, where there are only 4–9 materials, the comparisons are corrected for multiple testing (Holm–Bonferroni), and none survives. The explorer therefore labels the best number in a comparison as the "lowest observed error", never as a recommendation.

How were numerical and implementation failures investigated?

With checks that run on every model whatever its results look like, because errors that make results look better are less likely to be noticed. Three examples:

  • Invariance: a structure's energy must not depend on what else is in its batch or on the order of its atoms. MatterSim's kUPS export failed this, and is not used.
  • Consistency: the stress a model reports must equal the derivative of its own energy. This caught two frame-convention bugs in this project's own stress code before any result was published.
  • Determinism: rerunning a calculation must give the same answer. That exposed the Γ-mode defect in the phonon step, which made repeated runs scatter by up to ±8 %.

Molecular dynamics, the originally planned route to thermal expansion, was checked the same way. A run at fixed volume showed kUPS's isotropic barostat holding the pressure about 0.8 GPa below its target for copper at 650 K, which would bias thermal expansion, so the quasi-harmonic method was used instead.

What was fixed before results, and what changed afterwards?

The grading thresholds (lattice good ≤ 1 %, okay ≤ 3 %; bulk modulus 10 % and 25 %; thermal expansion 20 % and 40 %), the experimental references and the temperature-window rule were fixed before any model result was compared with them. Every later change is dated and explained in the configuration file:

  • Before any thermal result existed: the lower temperature bound, the MD run lengths, and the switch from MD to the quasi-harmonic method.
  • After a first quasi-harmonic pass, from phonon diagnostics only: how dynamical stability is judged.
  • After results had been compared with experiment, as a bug fix rather than a tuning: excluding the Γ-point acoustic modes. The 300 K comparison was also added after the first pass, as a secondary measure; the window average remains the pre-registered one.
  • Four quality-control rules for static properties were added after the first static results were inspected, for example excluding structures that change crystal form. They apply identically to every model and every excluded case is reported.

The six cases in the thermal-failure study were chosen after the failures were seen, so that study explains those cases; it is not a test of general rules.

What is measured, and what is inferred?

Lattice constants, bulk moduli and thermal-expansion coefficients are compared with measured values, each stored with its source and a short quote. Some references are derived from measurements, such as a bulk modulus averaged from published elastic constants, and are labelled as derived. In the thermal-failure study the experimental Grüneisen parameter is inferred from the measured expansion, stiffness and heat capacity, so it is not an independent check; only a few mode-resolved values are measured directly.

How good are the references themselves?

A citation is not enough: a reference also needs the right definition, suitable conditions and an honest uncertainty. Every reference is audited in data/reference_quality.yaml. Core references are primary measurements or critically evaluated compilations with stated conditions; extended ones are weaker: provisional compilations, thin films, single secondary sources or unresolved conflicts. Results are reported for both tiers, and no conclusion changes between them.

The audit changed two things. InP's and NiSi's expansion references are single values averaged over wide temperature ranges, so they are no longer used as 300 K values. And HfO₂'s and ZrO₂'s volumes are built from axial data that miss the change of the monoclinic angle, so they moved to the extended tier until the full expansion tensor can be used. Each reference's own uncertainty, where its source gives one, now feeds the statistics. Bulk moduli mix adiabatic and isothermal measurements; converting the adiabatic ones moves them by a median of 0.8 %, which changes no average by more than 0.35 points. The audited references are released as a standalone dataset.

How can someone reproduce the exact comparison?

Every stored result carries a fingerprint of the code, settings and inputs that produced it, and the analysis refuses to mix results from different versions. The exact package versions and the checksum of every model file are committed, so a rerun can be checked against what was published. Three routes, from reading the results to rerunning a calculation, are on the Reproduce page.
